\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Every compressed dataset carries an implicit contract: space is saved now, and
paid back later, at the moment somebody wants to use the data. The payment is
usually invisible because it is small relative to everything else. When a dataset
is a few gigabytes, expanding it costs a few seconds and the expanded form fits in
memory, so compression is purely a win and the decompress step is an
implementation detail.

That contract breaks when the expanded form no longer fits. At that point the
decompression step stops being a preamble to the computation and becomes the
dominant term: the system must stream hundreds of gigabytes off storage, rebuild
an in-memory representation larger than the input, and only then begin the work
that the user actually asked for. The compressed archive, meanwhile, sits unused
during the entire computation. It was treated as a shipping container, opened once
and discarded, rather than as a representation anything could run on.

This paper takes one workload where that break has already happened and asks what
changes if the compressed container is treated as the substrate the queries
execute on. The workload is pangenome analytics.

\paragraph{The workload.}
A pangenome graph represents the genomes of many individuals in one structure:
nodes are DNA segments, edges are admissible adjacencies, and each haplotype is
recorded as an ordered, orientation-signed walk through the
nodes~\cite{eizenga2020pangenome,liao2023draft}. Pangenomes are replacing the
single linear reference genome across human genomics because they remove reference
bias~\cite{lin2024measuring}, and they are exchanged as Graphical Fragment
Assembly (GFA) text~\cite{gfaspec}, the format that graph builders such as
Minigraph--Cactus~\cite{hickey2024pangenome} and PGGB~\cite{garrison2024building}
emit and that downstream tools consume.

The format scales badly, and it scales badly in a specific, structural way. Graph
topology grows sublinearly as haplotypes are added, because shared sequence
collapses onto shared nodes. Traversals do not: each haplotype's walk is written
out explicitly, so the traversal records grow nearly linearly with cohort size and
come to dominate everything else, exceeding $90\%$ of the file and, on some
chromosome graphs, $98\%$~\cite{heringer2025human}. Successive releases of the
Human Pangenome Reference Consortium graph illustrate the trajectory: $48$\,GB,
then $358$\,GB, then $369$\,GB of GFA text, with cohort sizes still an order of
magnitude below where population studies are heading~\cite{wang2022human}.

Traversal records are also the most compressible part of the file, precisely
because human haplotypes are nearly identical: the same node-ID substrings recur
across samples at every scale. Domain-specific compressors exploit
this~\cite{siren2022gbz,heringer2025human,gfaz2026}, and the best of them reduce a
$369$\,GB graph to roughly $4.5$\,GB. Storage, in other words, is a solved problem
for this workload.

Using the data is not. Every analysis a biologist runs on a pangenome today begins
by rebuilding something at least as large as the GFA. \cmd{vg deconstruct} loads
the graph and a snarl decomposition to emit
variants~\cite{garrison2018variation,hickey2020genotyping}; \cmd{odgi} builds a
succinct whole-graph index before any of its operations~\cite{guarracino2022odgi};
Panacus parses path and walk records into item tables before computing a growth
curve~\cite{parmigiani2024panacus}. On the two largest HPRC graphs, two of these
three cannot load the input at all on a $503$\,GiB machine, and the third needs
four hours and $330$\,GB of resident memory to answer a question whose entire
output is a few thousand integers. The $4.5$\,GB container is never in the loop.

\paragraph{The observation.}
Two properties of this workload make the materialization avoidable. First, the
dominant component of the file, the traversals, is already stored in a form that
can be walked: a grammar-compressed integer stream in which rule expansion is a
local, bounded-depth array lookup. Second, the analyses that consume pangenomes
are overwhelmingly \emph{path-centric folds}. They do not mutate the graph and
they do not need random node-to-step access. They scan traversals in order and
reduce node visits into an accumulator that is small: a coverage histogram, a
window-by-sample matrix, an allele table anchored to a reference path.

A fold over a stream does not need the stream materialized. It needs the elements
delivered in order, and a decoder that can produce them without global state. That
is exactly what a grammar-compressed traversal column can supply, which means the
compressed container is not merely storable but \emph{computable}, and the
expansion step everyone pays for is not required by the computation. It is
required only by the interface the existing tools present.

\paragraph{This paper.}
We build \sys, an execution engine that evaluates pangenome analyses directly on a
grammar-compressed container. A query decompresses the entropy-coded blocks it
needs, streams the still-grammar-compressed traversal columns through a templated
visitor, and merges per-thread accumulators. Nothing expands into a materialized
list of path steps, and no whole-graph object is ever constructed.

The consequence is a reversal of the usual storage trade-off. In the conventional
arrangement, a higher compression ratio means a longer wait before the computation
starts, so ratio trades against access latency. Here the compressed columns are
what the query reads and what its inner loop walks, so a higher ratio means fewer
bytes touched per unit of biological work. Compression stops being a tax on access
and becomes a multiplier on it: on the $369$\,GB HPRC graph, an $83\times$
reduction in bytes is what makes an analysis that took four hours take
$41$ seconds.

We do not claim this for graph workloads in general, and we are explicit about the
boundary. Operations requiring arbitrary node-to-step queries, graph editing, or
topology rewriting are better served by materialized graph toolkits, and we say so
in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. What we claim is that a large, ubiquitous, and
growing class of pangenome analytics falls on the streaming side of that line, and
that for this class the compressed container is the right thing to compute on.

\paragraph{Relationship to prior work on the container.}
The container format and its compressor are prior work, published
separately~\cite{gfaz2026}, and are \emph{not} a contribution of this paper. We
recap only the properties the engine depends on (Section~\ref{sec:container}) and
we do not re-evaluate compression ratio or compressor throughput as results.
Section~\ref{sec:principles} instead distills, from that format and from the
engine we built on it, the properties a compressed format must have for queries to
run on it at all, which is the part of this work we expect to transfer to other
domains.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=3pt,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{A characterization of computable compression.} Five properties
        (Section~\ref{sec:principles}) that separate a compressed format a query
        engine can execute on from one that must be expanded first, together with
        an account of where common storage formats sit against them.
  \item \textbf{A streaming execution engine over compressed traversals}
        (Section~\ref{sec:engine}): a decode primitive whose cost is linear in
        emitted elements and requires no side table, a memory model that bounds
        residency to the compressed columns plus a query accumulator, a bounded
        rule-leaf cache that trades memory for repeated expansion under an
        explicit budget, and a parallel schedule in which synchronization never
        touches the decode path.
  \item \textbf{\cmd{deconstruct}: variant projection without a graph}
        (Section~\ref{sec:deconstruct}). Reference-relative variant calling is
        the most consequential and the most topology-sensitive pangenome
        derivation, and the one apparently least suited to streaming. We give a
        two-phase algorithm that separates an $O(V{+}E)$ topology-only site
        discovery pass from a compressed-traversal allele fold, handles
        inversions in both phases, and reproduces the established tool's output
        at $\sim$$99.99\%$ position concordance without a materialized graph or a
        general snarl index.
  \item \textbf{An evaluation that measures the storage argument, not only the
        speedup} (Section~\ref{sec:eval}): end-to-end cost including I/O and from
        cold cache, a direct comparison against the obvious alternative of
        expanding to fast local storage first, sensitivity of query time to
        compression ratio, and thread scaling. Against \cmd{vg deconstruct},
        Panacus, and \cmd{odgi pav}, \sys is $15$--$613\times$ faster with
        $3$--$25\times$ less memory, and it answers questions on $358$--$369$\,GB
        graphs that the baselines cannot load.
  \item \textbf{A negative result we report rather than omit.} One of the six
        analyses, presence/absence variation, builds an uncompressed node-to-group
        join index. That accumulator, not the traversal store, exceeds machine
        memory at whole-genome scale, which locates the real limit of the design:
        computing over compression bounds the \emph{data} you hold, and says
        nothing about the state your query chooses to build
        (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
